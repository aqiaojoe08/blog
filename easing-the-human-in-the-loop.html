Easing the Human in the Loop: Review the Boundaries, Delegate the Details

An agent can write a change in a language its reviewer barely knows. Reading such a change line by line does not work, and line-by-line reading was never the best use of the person anyway. Delegate the low-level details to the agent. Keep the person in the loop where the agent is weak: the context going in, and the boundaries and the user experience coming out. Two agent skills move the review up to that level.

2,700 words~12 min read2026-11-22

In short

  • An agent can write a change in a language its reviewer does not know, such as Rust. Read line by line, that change leaves the reviewer two bad options: approve what they do not understand, or block on details they cannot judge.
  • Split the review by level. Delegate the low-level details to the agent, the compiler, and the linters. Keep the person where the agent is weak: the context going in, and the boundaries and the user experience coming out.
  • Going in, a blind-spot reviewer carries people’s considerations down to the code: plain-language rules, checklists, or links to design docs. It flags only gaps it can point to, and drafts comments that a person edits and publishes.
  • Coming out, a change visualizer lifts the diff to the level of a structured spec: what users see and what crosses components, before and after. A reviewer can judge it without reading the language.
  • The same views keep the discussion between people at the high level. Its conclusions belong in the spec, where the next review picks them up.

A change in a language you do not read

A change lands in your review queue. An agent wrote it, mostly in Rust, and you have never written Rust. The diff has almost 300 changed lines of Result, ?, Arc<Mutex<…>>, and lifetimes. You cannot tell whether a .clone() is a mistake or a requirement. Read line by line, the change leaves you two bad options: approve what you do not understand, or block on details you cannot judge.

Yet most of what matters in this review does not need Rust. Does the user see the right thing? Does the payment service call the card network in the right order, with the right key? Does the retry follow the team’s policy? Do older clients still work? These questions need context, and a view of the change at the right level. They do not need the language.

This is becoming the normal case. Anthropic’s engineers still review every change to Scion, the source control system they built with Claude, “but they now examine the intent of the change as opposed to reading every line of code” (Anthropic). A GitHub product manager told The Register that there are “more PRs for maintainers to review than ever before,” which “puts strain on workflows built around reading and commenting on every submission.” Maintainers said they are uncomfortable approving pull requests they do not fully understand (The Register). In March, Jazzband, a Python community that gave every member push access, announced that it would shut down. It cited the flood of AI-generated pull requests, and a report that only one in ten of them meets project standards (Jazzband).

Handing the whole review to an agent does not solve it either. Django’s contributing guide asks contributors not to request automated AI reviews on its pull requests, because they “do not replace human review and often generate noise that distracts maintainers” (Django). Anthropic found that its own adversarial reviews could be myopic, and its engineers had to steer Claude away from over-engineering in response to hypothetical concerns.

The person has to stay in the loop. The way to ease their part is to move them to the level where they add the most, and to hand everything below that level to the agent.

Split the review by level

The agent that wrote the Rust knows Rust better than you do: its ownership rules, its error handling, its idioms, and its standard library. The compiler, linters such as Clippy, and the tests check much of the rest. Delegate those details.

What the agent does not know is what the code does not say: why the system is built this way, what the design doc decided, what broke last time, and what a customer will notice. That knowledge sits with people, and so does the judgment of whether a change is right.

So the person takes part at two points. Going in, they name the context and the blind spots that the agent cannot see. Coming out, they judge the change from a view at the level of a structured spec: what users see and what crosses between components, before and after. The second point is the harder one. A study of code review at Microsoft found that “code and change understanding is the key aspect of code reviewing,” and that most of developers’ needs for understanding a change were not met by the tools of the time (Bacchelli and Bird).

Two agent skills serve the two points: a blind-spot reviewer going in, and a change visualizer coming out. This post describes them by their principles, which carry over to any code host and any agent.

Going in: name the blind spots

The blind-spot reviewer carries people’s context down to the code. The author or the reviewer names considerations in plain language. The skill checks every changed file against each one, and drafts comments for the gaps it finds. The considerations need no Rust: “Retries MUST use the shared backoff policy” and “Existing clients MUST keep working” apply to code in any language. Its principles:

  • Look where the rules cannot. The skill runs after the rule checks and skips what they cover. It looks for concerns that need context, such as a landmine in a legacy system, a helper that already exists in another repository, or a break in backward compatibility.
  • Let people name the blind spots. Considerations come as free text, a checklist, or links to design docs and decision records. A link works better than pasted text: the skill reads the current page, so the review stays in sync with the doc. With no input, the skill still runs an open scan.
  • Flag only gaps it can point to. A finding needs a concrete, observable gap in the changed lines, at a file and a line range. A concern that is only possible gets dropped. Google’s static analysis team lists reports where the bug is “theoretically possible, but the problem does not actually manifest in practice” among the reasons developers ignore tools. It holds code review checks to fewer than 10% effective false positives: findings that developers take no positive action on (Sadowski et al.). Anthropic’s reviews that chased hypothetical concerns show the cost of the opposite.
  • Name the consideration in every finding. Each comment starts with the consideration it belongs to, such as the retry policy or backward compatibility. A reader who does not know the language still knows what is at stake.
  • Take severity from the source’s own words. A consideration that says MUST, MUST NOT, REQUIRED, or SHALL makes a gap blocking. SHOULD, SHOULD NOT, RECOMMENDED, MAY, or no keyword makes it a suggestion. These are the keywords of RFC 2119, which many design docs already use, so severity is predictable. Google’s review guide asks reviewers to label severity for a related reason: without labels, “authors may interpret all comments as mandatory” (Google).
  • Ask, then suggest. Each comment asks a question, such as “What happens when the card network is overloaded?”, and ends with a concrete fix. Conventional Comments gives the same advice: pair an issue with a suggestion, and ask a question when you are not sure the problem exists. The question is for the person. The suggested code is for whoever makes the fix, often an agent.
  • One root cause, one comment. Findings at the same lines merge into one comment. A finding with the same root cause and fix as another is dropped.
  • Write like a peer. At most one hedge, and only when suggesting. No filler words such as “basically” or “potentially”. State facts about the code directly: “this leads to X”, not “this could lead to X”. One point per comment.
  • Cap the volume. Past a cap, ten by default, the skill posts no inline comments, only a summary. That many findings call for a conversation with the author. A summary is added when there are five or more comments. Clean files get no comments, and a clean change gets nothing.
  • Draft, and let a person publish. Every comment is saved as a draft. The person edits, keeps, or deletes each one, and publishes the rest under their own name, so a person stays accountable for what the review says.
  • Review the spec before the code. A spec-only mode reviews only the Markdown files in a change. In spec-driven development, the spec is reviewed before the code exists, and that is where a gap costs least to fix.

For the Rust change, a drafted comment looks like this:

Blocking · Retry policy. The design doc says retries MUST use the shared backoff policy. The new retry in charge.rs calls the card network again right away. What happens when the card network is overloaded? Suggestion: wrap the second call in the shared backoff helper, and add a test that checks the delay.

A reviewer can judge that comment without reading a line of Rust.

Coming out: judge the change at the spec level

The change visualizer lifts the diff to the level where a person judges it. It reads the change’s description, its commit messages, and its diff, and writes one self-contained page: what the user sees and the calls between components, before and after, and the lines changed per file. Together, the views read like a structured spec of the change. A reviewer can check the boundaries and the user experience on that page without reading the language. Its principles:

  • Explain the change at its boundaries. A reviewer first needs two things: what a user will see, and what crosses between components. Internal logic matters too, but a reviewer judges it by its effect at those boundaries.
  • Draw only what the diff supports. Every string, label, call, and arrow on the page comes from the diff or the code it touches. When a hunk is not enough to understand a function, the skill reads the whole file at the base commit, for a few files at most. It never invents behavior.
  • Take the why from the commits. Commit messages often say why a change was made and how it was tested. The page uses them instead of guessing.
  • Sort files by role. Each file goes into one group: user-facing surface (UI, CLI output, API response shapes, emails), boundary (handlers, clients, queues, database access, events, and config that routes traffic), internal logic, tests, or docs. The groups decide which views to draw.
  • Draw a view only when it carries meaning. The UX view shows what the user sees before and after, with each changed region marked and captioned. The interaction view is a pair of sequence diagrams, before and after, with each actor named after a real component and each call marked as added, removed, or changed. The file chart, with lines removed to the left and lines added to the right, is always there. An empty view is left out, with one line that says why, such as “No user-facing change: refactor of internal parsing only.”
  • Lead with the view that explains most, and end with a checklist. The page opens with its most telling view, or with the one the reviewer asks for. It ends with two to four concrete points to verify, each raised by a view, such as “the retry is added without a backoff”.
  • Make one page that stands alone. All CSS and SVG are inline, and the page fetches nothing from the network. Colors follow the reader’s theme and mean only added, removed, or changed. Every diagram has a label for screen readers.
  • Stay read-only, and redact. The visualizer never comments on, approves, or revises the change. Secrets, credentials, and customer data in the diff show up as [redacted].
  • Scale down on huge changes. Past about 40 files, the skill still charts every file, but draws the other views only for the ten files that changed most, and says so.

Keep the discussion at the high level

The views and the drafted comments also set the agenda for the people. When the author and the reviewer talk, they discuss what the views show. Should the retry live in the payment service? What does the user see while it waits? What does an older client get? Lifetimes are not on the agenda, because the agent and the compiler own them. A reviewer who is curious about a detail asks the agent, not the author.

The order of the work keeps it that way. Rule checks run first, on every change. The author runs the blind-spot reviewer before publishing, with the considerations they know, and fixes the gaps before anyone else looks. After publishing, the reviewer reads the visualization, runs the blind-spot reviewer with their own considerations, and edits the drafts before publishing them. At each step, the person receives a conclusion at the spec level instead of a diff to decode.

When the discussion settles a question, write the answer where the next review will find it: in the spec or the design doc. A link to that doc becomes a consideration for the next blind-spot review, so the decision is checked on every later change instead of living in one comment thread. Over time, the spec carries the team’s decisions, which is also why it pays to rebase the spec instead of the code when changes conflict.

Easing the human in the loop comes down to placing the person well. Delegate the details to the agent, which knows the language. Bring the person’s context in through considerations, and bring the change out through views at the spec level. The person’s attention then goes where it matters most: the boundaries, the user experience, and the decision. The aim is a review that is fast and still holds the bar.
