Faster Decisions in Complex Systems with LLMs and World Models

An LLM proposes a small set of strategies. An optimizer fills in the details. A world model tests what happens next.

~4 min read2026-11-14

A robot needs to move a cup past an obstacle. It could go around the left side, go around the right, or lift the cup over it. A business facing a supplier delay has a similar decision to make. It could transfer stock, expedite a shipment, or reduce a promotion. In both cases, the high-level choice changes which detailed decisions are worth considering.

The challenge is that those detailed decisions interact. A robot’s approach changes the feasible joint trajectories. A promotion change alters demand and therefore the best transfer quantity. When a system has to act quickly, jointly exploring every route, quantity, timing, and sequence can consume the time available to act.

I propose giving the system two levels of planning. Use an LLM to propose a few distinct ways to respond, then optimize and simulate within each one. The LLM helps decide where to search. The optimizer and world model determine what those choices would mean in practice.

How companies make complex decisions

When a company faces an unfamiliar problem, outside consultants can suggest a few approaches based on what they’ve seen elsewhere. The people who run the business know the local constraints. They use internal data, simulations, or a small pilot to test the promising options. If a test exposes a constraint, the team revises the proposal. The business owner decides which trade-offs to accept.

The same division of work applies here. The LLM proposes strategies, much like an outside consultant. The optimizer works out the details, and the world model tests each plan using data and constraints from the system itself. An agent or a business owner then chooses what to execute within their authority.

1. Define the decision before proposing actions

Capture what the system knows at the moment of decision. Include current state, the triggering change, the objective, the time available, and the constraints that every plan must satisfy.

For a robot, that means the observed scene, joint positions, the estimated grip, and limits on clearance, speed, and tilt. For a supplier delay, it means inventory, open orders, the revised arrival date, and business rules such as minimum availability. Keep a reference plan for comparison, such as a standard motion-planning policy or accepting the delay while keeping the promotion as scheduled.

2. Turn candidate strategies into smaller search problems

Ask the LLM for a short list of strategies using actions that the system supports. Each candidate specifies which decisions can vary, their allowable ranges, and which decisions stay fixed. Validate those fields against the action schema and policy before passing them to a solver.

Decision Candidate strategy What the solver still chooses
Move a cup around an obstacle Pass to the left while keeping the cup upright Waypoints, joint trajectories, and speed within the allowed corridor
Respond to a supplier delay Keep the promotion and combine a transfer with partial expediting How much to transfer, how much to expedite, and when to dispatch each

A robot can compare left, right, and overhead corridors without asking a single solve to explore all three geometries. A supply chain optimizer can compare plans with fixed promotion terms without trying every possible price. Within a candidate, the solver still handles coupled variables together. Changing the robot’s waypoint affects several joints at once. Expediting more inventory can reduce the transfer required.

The expected speedup comes from fixing irrelevant choices and bounding the remaining ones. A shortlist alone saves little if every candidate leaves the same variables open.

3. Simulate the consequences of each executable plan

A world model takes an initial state, a sequence of actions, and assumptions about uncertainty. It advances the system through time and returns the resulting states and outcomes. The solver can use a cheap approximation while searching, then send its best feasible plans to a more detailed simulator. For small problems, the simulator can also score actions directly during the search.

In robotics, advance the simulation in short time steps using the commanded trajectory and a model of the robot and scene. Check collisions, joint limits, cup tilt, and completion time. Repeat with plausible errors in the estimated obstacle position or grip. During execution, the controller uses sensor feedback to track the trajectory. If the scene changes materially, the system can stop safely and plan again from the updated state.

In supply chain, advance the simulation by day. Apply scheduled arrivals and transfers, estimate demand under each plan’s promotion settings, fulfill orders, and carry forward remaining inventory. Compare stockouts, contribution margin, and freight cost. Use the same underlying demand and lead-time scenarios across plans so the comparison reflects the actions taken.

Evaluate all candidates against the same objectives and hard constraints. A plan that looks favorable on average can still fail an important constraint under the required test conditions.

4. Select, execute, and learn from the outcome

Rank feasible plans against the decision policy. The agent can select a plan within its delegated authority. A person makes the call when the trade-off falls outside that authority. If no candidate passes, the system can use the simulator’s feedback for one more proposal round, fall back to an established policy, or allocate more time to search. Save the assumptions and results so observed outcomes can inform the next decision.

How to tell whether it works

Compare the proposed method with a fixed playbook and with broader optimization under the same end-to-end time budget. Count the time spent generating candidates, solving, and simulating. Measure time to the first feasible plan, time to the final decision, and the quality of the selected outcome. Where practical, use a longer search as a reference to estimate what the shortlist missed. Validate the simulator separately against observed outcomes for actions actually taken.

A short list can omit the best strategy, and a costly simulator can erase the time savings. The goal is to find a good, feasible decision before the opportunity to act passes. The method earns its place when it does that more reliably on the decisions the system actually faces.
