Easing the Human on the Loop When You Cannot Be Close to the Details

Agents now write code faster than anyone can read it. The lines I change each quarter went from about 4,700 at the start of 2025 to more than 190,000 at the start of 2026. I cannot stay close to the details, not because I do not want to, but because review does not scale to that speed. For systems with a high change blast radius, a person must still be on the loop. This is how I review the changes my agent makes: by their structure, not their lines.

In short

  • The lines I changed went from 4,667 in the first quarter of 2025 to 193,578 in the first quarter of 2026, about 40 times as many. Nobody can review at that speed and stay close to the details.
  • For systems with a high change blast radius, I stay on the loop. AutoSDE reviews and two agent skills handle the rest, and I review the change as structure, not as lines of code or prose.

You cannot stay close to the details

Code generation has sped up far more than reading has. My own code history shows it: I changed 77,755 lines in all of 2025 and 528,974 in 2026 through November 22. Anthropic sees the same at scale; its engineers “now examine the intent of the change as opposed to reading every line of code” (Anthropic). Nobody chose to drift away from the details. Review cannot scale to the speed of generation.

A new language widens the gap. In September, my agent ported a numerical component from Java to Rust, a language I am not familiar with: almost 21,000 lines of Rust in one change. Read line by line, it leaves two bad options: approve what I do not understand, or block on what I cannot judge. The questions that matter need no Rust: does the port give the same numbers as the Java code, where does it differ on purpose, and what happens on a path it does not support?

Systems with a high change blast radius, such as payments, identity, or shared infrastructure, cannot absorb a bad change, so a person must stay on the loop. In the loop, a person approves every line, which no longer scales. On the loop, the agent writes and fixes the code, and the person supplies the context, judges the results, and steps in when something is wrong.

How I review changes from my agent

The agent knows Rust better than I do. What it lacks is what the code does not say. So I split the work by level:

LevelWho owns itMy part
Details: syntax, idioms, ownershipThe agent, the compiler, the lintersDelegate. Ask the agent when curious.
Rules for the repository or the team: predefined, team-agreed, or agent-learnedAutoSDE reviews, on every changeAgree on a rule once, and stop repeating it
Context for this change: design decisions, past failures, customer needsPeopleName it as extra considerations, or link the doc that holds it
Boundaries and intent: what users see, what crosses components, whether the change is rightPeople, given the right viewJudge, and decide together

I take part at two points: going in, with context, and coming out, with judgment. I scale my part to the blast radius: an internal script may need only the rules, while a change to a shared system gets extra considerations, views, and a person who decides.

Review the structure, not the prose

Moving up from the code does not mean moving to prose. My team pointed out that a specification can be even harder to review than code, probably because code is more precise and concise: once you know its grammar, understanding follows from its structure. Prose is also linear, while a change is a tree or a graph, from the intent down to the code. So I review neither raw prose nor lines of code, but the structure, drawn as trees and graphs.

The port shows why. Its contract runs to 2,271 lines of Markdown, a tenth of the Rust and no easier to check. What I can judge in it is structure: a map from each Java type to its Rust type, the graph of module dependencies, and each two-way reference in the Java, broken in one direction.

Going in: rules, then blind spots

Context that holds for every change in a repository or a team belongs in rules. AutoSDE reviews apply them to each change there: predefined rules, rules the team agrees on, and rules an agent learns from the review comments people make most often. Nobody has to repeat them.

Context relevant to only this change goes to the blind-spot reviewer instead. I name my extra considerations in plain language, such as “The new retry MUST use the shared backoff policy.” It checks every changed file against each one and drafts comments. Its principles:

  • Look where the rules cannot. It runs after AutoSDE reviews, and looks for what needs this change’s context, such as backward compatibility.
  • Let people name the blind spots as free text, a checklist, or a link to a design doc, which stays in sync.
  • Flag only gaps it can point to, at a file and a line. A merely possible concern is dropped. Google holds its code review checks to under 10% effective false positives (Sadowski et al.).
  • Take severity from the source’s words. MUST makes a gap blocking, and SHOULD or MAY makes it a suggestion, as in RFC 2119.
  • Ask, then suggest, and name the consideration, so a reader without the language still knows what is at stake.
  • Stay quiet: one comment per root cause, a cap on the count, and nothing on clean files.
  • Draft, never publish. A person edits the drafts and publishes them under their own name.

On the same payment change, a draft reads:

Blocking · Retry policy. The design doc says retries MUST use the shared backoff. The new retry in charge.rs calls the card network again right away. What happens when it is overloaded? Suggestion: wrap the call in the shared backoff helper.

I can judge that comment without reading Rust.

Coming out: the change visualizer

The change visualizer draws part of that structure for a diff: one self-contained page whose views read like a structured spec of the change. Its principles:

  • Explain the change at its boundaries: what users see, and what crosses between components.
  • Draw only what the diff supports. Take the why and the testing from the commit messages, and never invent behavior.
  • Draw a view only when it carries meaning: a before-and-after of what users see, a pair of sequence diagrams, and a file chart that is always there. Say why a view is missing.
  • Lead with the most telling view, and end with two to four points to check.
  • Stay read-only and self-contained, with secrets redacted.

Keep the discussion on the structure

With my team, the views and the drafts set the agenda. We discuss whether the retry belongs in the service and what an older client gets, not lifetimes. Questions about details go to the agent.

When the discussion settles a question, I write the answer into the spec, or into a rule if it applies to every change in the repository or the team. Either way, every later review checks it.

Code will keep getting faster to write, and I will not get back to reading every line. For systems with a high change blast radius, keep a person on the loop, and make their part easy: delegate the details and the team’s rules, add this change’s context as extra considerations, and bring the change out as structure. Their attention then goes to the boundaries, the user experience, and the decision.
